There is no correct answer to "at what age should I start teaching Hangul." But there is a far more reliable measure than age: **whether the child has started paying attention to letters**. Two five-year-olds can be in completely different places. One points at a shop sign and asks "what does that say?" The other is still only responding to the pictures.

Getting the timing wrong costs you in one of two ways. Start too early and the child files letters away as "boring and hard." Start too late and the first year of elementary school becomes a scramble to keep up. That is why it is better to read the child's signals than to pick a birthday.

## Signs that it is fine to start

If you see three or more of these, the timing is reasonable.

- **Tells letters apart from pictures** — points a finger at the text in a book rather than the illustration
- **Shows interest in their own name** — spots the letters of their name somewhere else and is pleased about it
- **Hears sounds separately** — can answer a question like "what's the first sound in 사과 (sagwa, apple)?"
- **Distinguishes similar sounds** — hears that 방 (bang, room) and 빵 (ppang, bread) are different words
- **Controls a pencil or crayon** — draws circles and straight lines on purpose
- **Asks first** — comes to you with "how do you read this?"

The third and fourth items matter most. **Being able to hear a word broken into its separate sounds** is the real prerequisite for learning Hangul. Hangul writes sounds as symbols, so if a child cannot hear the sounds come apart, there is no route left except memorizing whole shapes. When that ability is there, learning moves noticeably faster.

And if the signals have not arrived yet, **sound games** work as a warm-up instead of letters. Word chains, hunting for words that start with the same sound, singing a familiar song with the words swapped out. It is a way of preparing for Hangul without showing the child a single letter.

## Two methods — whole-word and letter-based

Broadly, there are two ways to teach Hangul.

**The whole-word method (meaning-first)** has the child learn words as complete units. The block of letters spelling 사과 (sagwa, apple) gets remembered the way a picture would. It is easy to begin and the early wins come fast. The child reads several words within days, which is exciting for the parent too.

**The letter-based method (sound-first)** teaches consonants and vowels first, then combining them to read. Once a child knows ㄱ (g) and ㅏ (a), they can read 가 (ga) — and on the same principle they go on to read 나 (na) and 다 (da) by themselves. It is slower at the start, but the child can read syllables nobody taught them.

Here is how the commonly cited differences between the two break down.

| | Whole-word | Letter-based |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early pace | Fast | Slow |
| Unfamiliar letters | Cannot read them | Can work them out |
| Child's interest | Easy to hold | Hard to hold at first |
| Ceiling | Reads only as many words as memorized | Stalls until the combining principle clicks |

There is one example that comes up again and again as the classic limit of the whole-word method. A child who reads 김밥 (gimbap) as a unit cannot read 김 (gim) once you take it out on its own. The block of letters was stored as a picture, so the child has no idea what parts it was built from. Keep going down that road and the rate at which the child adds new words eventually hits a wall.

## So what should you actually do

Most of the teaching material I have looked through recommends a compromise: **put the letter-based method at the center and use whole-word as a support**. Hangul maps sounds to letters regularly, and it seems a waste not to use that regularity. There is also research showing that a program combining both methods produced gains in both reading and writing.

In practice you can order it like this.

1. **The child's name and family names as whole words** — this is for motivation. Being able to read and write their own name changes a child's attitude toward letters.
2. **Vowels first, then consonants** — vowels have simple sounds and you can check them by mouth shape, which makes them a good starting point.
3. **Practice combining with one consonant plus every vowel** — building 가·거·고·구·기 (ga, geo, go, gu, gi) from the single letter ㄱ (g) makes the combining principle stick.
4. **Plenty of syllables with no final consonant** — rush this and you will certainly get stuck later.
5. **Single final consonants → doubled → clustered** — starting with the frequently used single final consonants is the approach in a lot of workbooks.

For reference, modern Hangul has 27 final consonants in total (받침, batchim — the consonant that closes a syllable): 14 single, 2 doubled, and 11 clustered. The national curriculum does not officially prescribe a learning order, but working through the high-frequency single ones first is standard practice across workbooks.

## How much per day

Even in first-grade Korean language class, a substantial amount of time is set aside for Hangul. Under the 2015 revised national curriculum, Hangul instruction went from 27 hours to 68 hours, concentrated in the first semester of first grade. In other words, **this is material that school itself spends a semester teaching**.

If you are starting at home ahead of that, it is worth using that pace as your yardstick. Ten to fifteen minutes a day, four or five days a week, is enough. What matters is not the total hours but that a little happens every day. And when the child shows signs of being bored, it is better to just stop for the day. Hangul is learned over months, so skipping one day costs almost nothing — but making the child dislike it costs months.

## Summary

- Judge the starting point by the child's readiness signs, not their age. The ability to hear a word's separate sounds is the key one.
- If the signs are not there yet, warm up with sound games instead of letters.
- The safe compromise is a letter-based core, with whole-word used only for motivating words like names.
- Build a solid base of syllables with no final consonant before moving to single final consonants. Rushing here is the most common mistake.
- Ten to fifteen minutes a day, four or five days a week. When boredom shows up, stopping for the day is the better trade.
