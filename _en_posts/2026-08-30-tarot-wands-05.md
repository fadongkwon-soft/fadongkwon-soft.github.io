Five people tangled together holding five wands — the first thing most people see in that picture is a fight or a quarrel. Plenty of readings end with someone worried that drawing the Five of Wands means a serious conflict is on the way. Look the picture over slowly, though, and the scene is less a grim battle than a noisy scuffle, five people each swinging a wand in their own way.

## A closer look at the picture

- **Nobody is hurt and nobody is down** — the wands are clashing, yet no blood and no fallen figure is drawn anywhere. That is an important clue that this conflict is closer to competitive tension than to a destructive war.
- **Wands pointing in every direction** — the five are not in a formation with their movements matched; each one insists on his own style as he swings. Rather than collaboration having broken down completely, the accurate reading is a mix of several voices whose direction has not been coordinated yet.
- **The wide-open field** — a space with nowhere to hide and nowhere to escape also means the conflict is out in plain sight instead of festering behind someone's back.
- **The temperature of the faces** — the expressions are drawn closer to competitiveness or excitement than to fear. That is the thread that separates a scuffle near play from a genuine threat.

The number 5 is the place of conflict and lack, where the stability of the four breaks and each person's own way of doing things starts to collide. The harmony enjoyed in the Four of Wands goes on trial from here.

## Upright meaning

Upright, the Five of Wands points to the lively tension made when several people's ideas collide. That tension is not necessarily a bad sign, because a better direction sometimes gets filtered out in the back-and-forth between differing opinions. What matters is holding a line so it does not spill over into a fight about feelings.

## Reversed meaning

Reversed, the Five of Wands turns up with two different faces. One is avoidance, dodging the conflict entirely because facing it is unpleasant. The other is the end of a war of attrition, a long-running dispute finally dying down. Either way the point is the same: rather than fearing the clash itself, what is needed is the attitude that brings it to a healthy close.

## Readings by situation

### 💼 Business

Upright is a time of fierce competition with others in the same industry. Rather than getting pulled into a price war, differentiating on quality and service pays off over the long run. Reversed clears up a long-running industry dispute and opens the search for a path to coexistence, so looking for an amicable compromise with a competitor is one way to go.

### 🏢 Career

Upright is a time when tension pulls tight through clashes between teams or competition inside the company. Working it out through constructive discussion rather than an emotional fight helps in the end. Reversed points to draining factional disputes being settled and the team getting its unity back.

### 🤝 Relationships

Upright is a time when mild disagreements or a competitive streak surface easily among friends. An attitude that acknowledges each other's differences is needed so it does not spill into a fight about feelings. Reversed is a period when a draining quarrel with people you know gets tied off and peace returns, so setting your pride down a little and reaching out first is worth doing.

### 📚 Study & Exams

Upright is a time when heated discussion with your study group or friendly rivalry picks up. Turn that stimulus into positive motivation and it becomes the trigger that lifts your ability. Reversed points to a state where draining feelings from comparing yourself to others keep you from concentrating on the work itself, so moving the object of comparison from other people to yesterday's version of yourself works better.

## What the Five of Wands says makes conflict healthy

The Five of Wands sometimes gets confused with cards like the Tower or Death, since all three leave the impression that calm has been broken. Their character is clearly different, though. The Tower is the card that tears down and Death is the card that ends, and the Five of Wands is neither. In this scene of five people colliding there is no collapse and no ending. It is closer to a picture of living competition, different forces meeting and generating heat. Conflict is not all bad, and a reasonable amount of tension can be the raw material of growth — the Five of Wands is the card that makes you feel that firsthand rather than just know it in theory.
